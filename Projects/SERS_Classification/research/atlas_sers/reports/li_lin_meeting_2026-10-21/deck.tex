\begin{frame}{Since we last spoke (14 September)}
\textbf{Already reported:} the classical machine-learning baseline. Extra Trees identified the chemical on an instrument it had never seen with 68.5\% balanced accuracy (chance: 33\%).

\vspace{0.6em}
\textbf{New since then}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compact neural networks (CNNs), including versions trained to ignore instrument differences.
  \item Three preprocessing pipelines --- minimal, smoothing, baseline correction --- tested on every model.
  \item Your question from 1 September: do the substrates detect the analyte independently of the instrument?
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.6em}
\textbf{For discussion}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Paper 1: what this dataset supports, and what it still needs.
  \item Paper 2: the original VAE goal, and what a proper dataset for it looks like.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% -------------------------------------------------------------------------- 3
\begin{frame}{What we set out to do}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Original idea}\par\smallskip
Train a disentangled variational autoencoder (VAE) to split each spectrum into a chemical part and a substrate/instrument part.

\medskip
\textbf{Why this dataset cannot support it}
\begin{itemize}
  \item No pure-analyte reference spectra, so a ``chemical part'' cannot be checked.
  \item Chemicals, substrates and instruments are not crossed: 374 of 2,760 sample $\times$ substrate $\times$ instrument combinations were measured.
  \item Only 69 independently prepared samples.
\end{itemize}
\column{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Current focus}\par\smallskip
Your clarification: the trial asked whether the SERS substrates detect the analyte \emph{independently of the instrument}.

\medskip
\textbf{Main question}\par\smallskip
Can a model identify the chemical on an instrument --- and on samples --- that it never saw during training?

\medskip
\textbf{Follow-up questions}\par\smallskip
When does preprocessing help? Do neural networks add anything over classical methods? Which substrates hold up across instruments?
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Paper 2: the original VAE goal, with a designed dataset}
\textbf{Working title:} Separating analyte, substrate and instrument contributions in SERS spectra with structured variational autoencoders
\gapbox{The gap: no SERS dataset measures every chemical on every substrate and instrument, so the chemical's signal has never been separated from the rest.}

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.50\textwidth}
\textbf{What the model separates}
\begin{enumerate}\footnotesize
  \item Instrument response --- removed.
  \item Substrate background --- removed.
  \item The analyte's shared SERS signature --- \textbf{the real signal}.
  \item A substrate-specific change to the analyte (chemistry $\times$ substrate) --- measured.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{How it is tested}
\begin{itemize}\footnotesize
  \item Recover each signature; compare with pure Raman to see how much SERS alters it.
  \item Predict combinations deliberately left out of training.
  \item Compare against the Paper~1 methods.
\end{itemize}
\column{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Closest prior work}\par
\scriptsize
\begin{itemize}
  \item Fornasaro et al., \emph{Anal.\ Chem.} 2020: 15 labs, 6 SERS methods --- but one analyte; authors caution against extrapolating to other substrates.
  \item Mahmud et al., \emph{Spectrochim.\ Acta A} 2026: 20 analytes, 5 instruments --- but one substrate.
  \item Abram \& McCloskey, \emph{Biomolecules} 2023: a VAE separates instrument type --- in mass spectrometry.
  \item Lotfollahi et al., \emph{Mol.\ Syst.\ Biol.} 2023: predicts unseen drug $\times$ cell-type combinations --- a template from biology.
\end{itemize}
\bigskip
\textbf{Candidate journals}\par
\scriptsize Analytical Chemistry (ACS) \,$\cdot$\, Digital Discovery (RSC) \,$\cdot$\, Machine Learning: Science and Technology (IOP) \,$\cdot$\, Nature Communications (stretch) \,$\cdot$\, plus a Scientific Data descriptor for the dataset


\end{columns}
\src{Paper 1 comes first: it shows acquisition effects dominate and why separating them needs a crossed design. Literature checked 20 October 2026; repeat in Scopus before submission.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Paper 2: what a proper dataset needs}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.40\textwidth}
\centering
\scriptsize\textbf{Today: independent samples per chemical $\times$ substrate}\par\smallskip
\input{figures/coverage_chemical_substrate.tex}
\column{0.58\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{1. Fill the matrix.} Every analyte on every substrate and instrument, about 5 independent preparations per cell. Today H-Kit met only CWA chemicals and pSERS only Pills/Surfaces chemicals, so substrate and chemical cannot be separated.

\smallskip
\textbf{2. Ground truth for each effect.} Pure-analyte Raman references, substrate-only blanks, instrument intensity standards (e.g.\ NIST SRM 2241).

\smallskip
\textbf{3. Enough instruments.} $\geq$5--6, including $\geq$3 units of one brand, so ``works on a new instrument'' is tested more than a few times.

\smallskip
\textbf{4. The same preparation on every instrument.} The pairing shows the model directly what an instrument effect looks like.
\end{columns}
\takeaway{Illustrative design: (6 analytes $\times$ 3 concentrations + 1 blank) $\times$ 4 substrates $\times$ 5 preparations = 380 independent samples, each read on 5 instruments $\times$ 3 scans $\approx$ 5,700 spectra.}
\src{Theory agrees: without labelled, crossed factors, disentanglement cannot be learned (Locatello et al., ICML 2019); correlated factors stay entangled (Tr\"auble et al., ICML 2021).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: how much data, details}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.50\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Rule} (Zhang \& Gupta, 2000): $N$ samples $\times$ $M$ outputs give $N{\cdot}M$ constraints for $W$ weights.
$N{\cdot}M > W$: overdetermined, one stable fit. $N{\cdot}M < W$: underdetermined, many fits.


\smallskip
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
& $W$ & $N{\cdot}M$ & $W \div N{\cdot}M$\\
\midrule
CNN, one station & 208,691 & 54 & $\approx$3,900\\
VAE, NATO data & $\approx$100k & 27.5k & $\approx$3.6\\
VAE, Paper 2 design & 20--80k & 17.5--87k & $\approx$1\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\column{0.48\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Modern methods} (early stopping, convolutions, augmentation, pretraining, physics-based decoders) let compact models work below the classical limit. Our CNN was thousands of times underdetermined and still matched Random Forest --- but did not beat it.

\medskip
\textbf{Confidence comes from the test.} The 95\% range of an 80\% accuracy:

\smallskip
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
Independent test samples & Range\\
\midrule
19 (today's substrate tests) & $\pm$18 points\\
60 & $\pm$10 points\\
250 & $\pm$5 points\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{columns}
\takeaway{Plan $\approx$300--500 crossed preparations, $\geq$60 independent test samples per key comparison and $\geq$5--6 instruments. Size the final study from a $\approx$100-preparation pilot learning curve.}
\src{Ratio above 1 = underdetermined. $M$ = 3 for a three-chemical classifier; $\approx$46 for a spectrum (46 principal components hold 95\% of the variation). VAE sizes are assumptions.}

Independent sample & A separately prepared physical sample; repeat spectra of it are not independent.\\
Parameter & One adjustable number a model learns from data (a weight).\\
Extra Trees / Random Forest & Many decision trees voting; Extra Trees chooses its splits more randomly.\\
SVM & Support-vector machine: finds the boundary with the widest margin between chemicals.\\
CNN & Convolutional neural network: learns local spectral patterns such as peak shapes.\\
arPLS & Asymmetrically reweighted penalized least squares: estimates and subtracts a smooth background.\\
SG smoothing & Savitzky--Golay filter: local polynomial smoothing (11 points, degree 3).\\
Confusion matrix & Table of true versus predicted chemical.\\
\end{tabular}
